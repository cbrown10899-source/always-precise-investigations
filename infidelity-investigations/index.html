Why adultery evidence matters more in Virginia than most states

In Virginia, adultery is not just a private wound — it is a fault ground for divorce, and it can bear on issues like spousal support. That legal weight comes with a matching burden: Virginia courts require corroboration. Your own testimony, your screenshots, your certainty — on their own, these are generally not enough. What changes a case is independent, professional documentation: where they went, who they met, how long they stayed, on what dates, with video that carries its own time stamps.

That is the entire job of an infidelity investigation. Not confrontation, not drama — a clean, dated record built by a licensed third party with no stake in your marriage, prepared so your attorney can actually use it.

Nothing on this page is legal advice. How adultery affects divorce, support, or custody in your situation is a question for a Virginia family-law attorney — we work alongside them, not in place of them.

What we will not do — and why that protects you

Anyone offering to "get into their phone," clone their messages, or slap a GPS tracker on a car they have no authority over is offering you evidence that can be thrown out — and legal exposure to go with it. Virginia restricts electronic tracking and unauthorized access to accounts and devices. We do not trespass, intercept private communications, or access anyone's accounts. Lawful surveillance from public vantage points, properly documented, is what survives a courtroom — which is the only kind of evidence worth paying for.

Is adultery illegal in Virginia?

Virginia is one of the states where adultery remains on the books as a misdemeanor, though prosecutions are rare. Where it genuinely matters is divorce court: adultery is a fault ground for divorce in Virginia and can affect issues such as spousal support. How it applies to your case is a question for your attorney — what we supply is the documentation.

What proof of adultery do Virginia courts expect?

Virginia requires corroboration — a spouse's own testimony or suspicion is generally not sufficient on its own. Courts look for independent evidence that is clear and convincing: documented time together at private residences or hotels, patterns over time, and testimony from a licensed investigator who observed it. That corroboration is exactly what a professional surveillance file is built to provide.

Can you track my spouse's phone or put a GPS tracker on their car?

No — and you should hang up on anyone who says yes. Virginia law restricts electronic tracking of a vehicle you do not have authority over, and accessing someone's phone or accounts without consent is illegal. Evidence gathered that way can be excluded and can create legal exposure for you. Lawful physical surveillance, properly documented, is what holds up.

Will my spouse find out I hired an investigator?

No. Surveillance is covert by design and our relationship with you is confidential. Investigators work from unmarked vehicles, keep their distance, and never make contact with the subject. The goal is documentation without any change in your spouse's behavior.
