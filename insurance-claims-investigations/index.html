What a claims file actually needs

A claim rarely turns on whether an injury happened. It turns on the gap — if there is one — between the restrictions in the file and what the claimant does on an ordinary Tuesday. Closing that gap takes a dated record built by a licensed third party: what was observed, at what time, from where, for how long, on video that carries its own time stamps.

What makes that record useful is discipline about its limits. We report what an investigator observed, not what it implies about the claim. A day with nothing to show is written up as a day with nothing to show. Adjusters and defense counsel can work with that; what they cannot work with is a report that stretches, because the first time it stretches in a deposition, the entire file loses its weight — including the parts that were solid.

Nothing on this page is legal advice, and nothing here is a claim about any particular file. Coverage decisions, compensability and litigation strategy are for the carrier and its counsel — we supply the documentation they rely on.

What we will not do — and why it protects your file

Evidence is only worth what it survives. We do not trespass onto private property, record into a residence, place tracking devices on vehicles we have no authority over, access accounts or devices, or misrepresent ourselves to obtain financial or medical records. Every one of those shortcuts creates a suppression argument at best and exposure for the carrier at worst — and each of them takes a defensible file and turns it into the other side's exhibit.

Lawful observation from public vantage points, documented continuously and reported without embellishment, is slower and it is the only version that holds. Always Precise Investigations is licensed by Virginia DCJS and has worked Virginia claims since 2014.

Common questions from claims professionals

Do you handle both workers' compensation and auto liability claims?

Yes. The field work is the same discipline in both: establish what the claimant is actually doing, document it with dated video, and write it up so it survives scrutiny. Workers' compensation assignments tend to turn on activity versus stated restrictions, while auto and general liability assignments more often involve scene documentation, witness location and injury verification. We take both, and we take them from carriers, third-party administrators, self-insured employers and defense counsel.

Do you bill mileage and travel time on claims assignments?

No hidden mileage or travel fees anywhere in our service area — the same policy we apply to every case. You get a quote before work begins and the invoice matches it. For claims professionals working to a reserve, that predictability is usually the point: a surveillance budget that quietly doubles in travel charges is worse than no surveillance at all.

What do we receive at the end of an assignment?

A written activity report with a time-stamped narrative of what was observed hour by hour, supported by video and photographs, plus the raw footage. Negative results are reported exactly as plainly as positive ones — a day where the claimant did nothing observable is a finding, and writing it up as anything else would make the whole file worthless.

Will surveillance video hold up before the Virginia Workers' Compensation Commission?

That depends on how it was obtained and how it was documented, which is exactly why the method matters. Video shot from lawful vantage points, with continuous time stamps, an unbroken chain of custody and a licensed investigator who can testify to what they personally observed, is what stands up. Footage with unexplained gaps, or gathered in a way that invites a suppression argument, hands the other side a problem instead of solving yours.

Is it legal to record a claimant in Virginia?

Recording video of someone in a public place, where there is no reasonable expectation of privacy, is lawful in Virginia, and that is where surveillance is properly conducted. Virginia is a one-party consent jurisdiction for recording a conversation you are yourself part of. What is not lawful is trespassing onto private property, recording into a residence, or accessing devices and accounts. We do not do any of those things.

Can your investigator testify about what they observed?

Yes. The investigator who performed the surveillance is available for deposition and for hearing testimony about what they personally observed. That is one reason we keep the reporting narrow and factual — an investigator testifies far better to a dated record of observed behavior than to characterizations or conclusions about a claimant.
